# Chapter 13 — Transforming Society: AI and Machine Learning

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Artificial intelligence (AI) | Computer systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence. |
| Machine learning | A branch of AI where a system learns patterns from data rather than being given explicit rules. |
| Training data | The examples used to teach a machine-learning model. |
| Model | The pattern or rule set produced by training, used to make predictions. |
| Bias | A systematic unfairness in data or in a model's outputs. |
| Encryption | Encoding data with an algorithm so that only authorised parties can read it. |
| HTTPS | HTTP with encryption (TLS), protecting data in transit. |
| Digital currency | Money that exists only in digital form. |
| Cryptocurrency | A decentralised digital currency whose transactions are recorded on a blockchain. |
| Fiat currency | Money backed by a government rather than by a physical commodity. |
| Disruptive technology | Technology that changes an industry so much that existing businesses must adapt or fail. |
| Remote working | Working from a location other than the employer's premises, connected by technology. |

## Improving lives: health and genetics

Computing has changed medicine. Analysing a patient's genome can identify a rare genetic disease that would otherwise take years to diagnose; in one reported case, a baby's condition was identified by comparing genetic data with known variants. Screening can also look for genetic markers associated with conditions such as cystic fibrosis, allowing earlier advice and treatment.

The same technology makes gene editing possible. That raises ethical questions: editing embryos, altering inherited traits, and unequal access to treatment. The technical ability does not settle whether it should be used.

## Encryption and privacy

Encryption uses algorithms to scramble data so it cannot be read without the key. It protects messages, banking, medical records and industrial communications. HTTPS applies encryption to web traffic, so a visitor knows the connection is private and the site is authenticated.

Encryption protects data at rest as well as in transit: a stolen laptop with an encrypted disk does not expose its files. However, encryption also creates tension with law enforcement and security agencies, who may want access to communications. That is a social and legal debate, not just a technical one.

## The economy and currency

Online banking and card payments depend on secure networks. Digital currencies exist only electronically; cryptocurrencies add decentralisation, with transactions recorded on a blockchain rather than controlled by a central bank. They offer lower transaction costs and access without a bank account, but their value is volatile, they consume significant energy, and they can be used for illegal payments.

Fiat currency depends on trust in the issuing government and requires expensive infrastructure to move and secure. Digital money may reduce that overhead, but it also creates dependence on networks and electricity.

## Jobs and remote working

Computers have created job types that did not exist before: programmers, IT managers, web designers and social media roles. They have also removed jobs, particularly routine and manual work in retail and manufacturing, as automation and online shopping grew. Opinions differ on whether more jobs are created than lost, but the composition of work clearly changes, and the people affected are not always the ones who gain.

Remote working, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, allows work from anywhere with a connection. It offers flexibility and access to wider talent, but it can blur work and home life, disadvantage those with poor connectivity or unsuitable space, and reduce informal collaboration.

## Example

A hospital uses machine learning to flag patients at risk of a disease. The benefit is earlier treatment; the risk is that the training data may under-represent some groups, so the model could be less accurate for them. The ethical response is not to abandon the tool but to test its accuracy across groups, explain its limits to clinicians, and keep a human decision in the loop. The technology is only part of the system; the people and processes around it determine whether it helps.

## AI, precisely

**Artificial intelligence** is the science and engineering of making systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence — perception, language, decision-making, generalisation.

- **Narrow AI** performs one task and cannot transfer its skill: a spam filter, a recommendation system.
- **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** would solve varied problems across domains and learn autonomously; it does not exist today.
- **Generative AI** produces new content — text, images, video, music, code — from a prompt, and can **hallucinate**: state confident but false information.

Machine learning is the branch of AI where patterns are learned from **training data** rather than programmed as explicit rules. The output is a **model**, and its quality depends on the data: biased, incomplete or unrepresentative data produces biased, incomplete or unrepresentative predictions.

## Computer vision

Computer vision classifies and interprets images. A model may output a label with a confidence, such as "cat: 94%", meaning it is 94% confident that region contains a cat — not that 94% of the image is a cat.

Applications: medical imaging for early diagnosis; autonomous vehicles detecting pedestrians; checkout-free retail; crop monitoring; facial recognition; manufacturing inspection; wildlife monitoring; augmented reality.

Bias arises when training images under-represent groups. Mitigations named in the schemes: a **diverse and representative** dataset, a **larger** dataset, diversity in the development team, continuous **bias auditing**, and keeping a **human in the loop** for consequential decisions.

## Emerging technologies

| Technology | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Quantum computing | Solves certain problems far faster; could transform medicine, materials and cryptography. | Expensive, unstable; threatens current encryption. |
| Edge computing | Processes data near the source, reducing latency and keeping sensitive data local. | Limited processing and storage; many devices are complex to manage. |
| Internet of Things | Convenience and efficiency through connected sensors and devices. | Privacy and security risks; many vulnerable entry points. |
| Biometric authentication | Strong, convenient security using fingerprints or faces. | Irreversible if compromised; spoofing, false positives and false negatives. |
| Blockchain | Decentralised, transparent, tamper-resistant records. | Public ledger can be traced; very high energy use. |

**Cloud computing** offers scalability, accessibility and backup, but depends on connectivity and transfers control of data to a provider.
