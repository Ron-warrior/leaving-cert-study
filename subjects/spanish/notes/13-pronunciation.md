# Pronunciation, spelling and the sounds that cost marks

Spanish spelling is consistent: once you know the rules, you can pronounce almost any word. The exam does not test a perfect accent, but clear vowels, correct stress and confident delivery make a strong impression in the oral and help you recognise words in the aural.

## The five vowels

Spanish has five short, clear vowels. Unlike English, they do not glide into a second sound.

| Letter | Sound | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a | ah | `casa` |
| e | eh | `mesa` |
| i | ee | `vino` |
| o | oh | `ropa` |
| u | oo | `luna` |

Keep the vowels short. `No` is not “noh-oo”; `sí` is not “see-y”. A common English-speaker error is to turn final vowels into diphthongs.

### Minimal pairs to hear and say

- `pero` (but) / `perro` (dog)
- `papa` (potato / pope) / `papá` (dad)
- `si` (if) / `sí` (yes)
- `esta` (this) / `está` (is)
- `el` (the) / `él` (he)
- `solo` (alone / only) / `sólo` (only, older spelling)
- `tu` (your) / `tú` (you)
- `mas` (but, literary) / `más` (more)

## Consonants that need attention

### b and v

In most varieties, `b` and `v` sound the same. Do not make a strong English “v” with your teeth on your lip.

- `vino` sounds like “bino”.
- `vivir` has two soft b/v sounds.
- Use context, not the spelling, to tell words apart in listening.

### c, z and s

- In most of Spain, `c` before `e`/`i` and `z` sound like the `th` in “think”: `gracias`, `cena`, `zapato`.
- In Latin America and parts of southern Spain, they sound like `s`: `grasias`, `sena`, `sapato`.
- `c` before `a`, `o`, `u` is a hard `k`: `casa`, `comer`, `curso`.
- `g` before `e`/`i` is a harsh `h`: `gente`, `girasol`. Before `a`, `o`, `u` it is hard: `gato`, `goma`.

### h, j and ll

- `h` is always silent: `hola`, `hacer`, `hoy`.
- `j` sounds like a strong English `h`: `jugar`, `trabajo`, `viaje`.
- `ll` sounds like a `y` in most varieties: `llamar`, `calle`, `paella`. In parts of Argentina and Uruguay it can sound like `sh`.
- `y` at the end of a word sounds like a vowel: `hoy`, `rey`.

### r and rr

- A single `r` between vowels is a light tap, similar to the `tt` in some English accents: `pero`, `caro`.
- `rr` is a rolled/trilled sound: `perro`, `carretera`.
- A word starting with `r` is also trilled: `rápido`, `rosa`.

### d and t

Spanish `d` and `t` are softer and more dental than in English. Do not explode them with a puff of air. At the end of a word, `d` can be very soft: `Madrid`, `usted`.

## Stress and written accents

Stress is part of the word, not an optional decoration. The rules:

1. Words ending in a vowel, `n` or `s` stress the **second-to-last** syllable: `ca-sa`, `ha-blan`, `ex-a-mes`.
2. Words ending in any other consonant stress the **last** syllable: `ha-blar`, `ciu-dad`, `es-pa-ñol`.
3. A written accent marks an exception: `teléfono`, `está`, `música`, `corazón`.
4. Accents also distinguish meaning: `si` / `sí`, `el` / `él`, `tu` / `tú`, `esta` / `está`.

### Say these aloud

- `hablo` (I speak) / `habló` (he spoke)
- `canto` (I sing) / `cantó` (he sang)
- `practico` (I practise) / `practicó` (he practised)
- `ingles` is wrong; `inglés` is correct.
- `espanol` is wrong; `español` is correct.

In the oral, a missing accent rarely changes comprehension, but in writing it can change the tense. In the aural, stress can change the word entirely.

## Linking and rhythm

Spanish tends to link words together:

- `¿Dónde está?` can sound like “dondestá”.
- `mi amigo` can sound like “miamigo”.
- `la universidad` can sound like “launiversidad”.

Do not panic if you cannot separate every word in the recording. Listen for the stressed content words and the overall phrase. In your own speech, link naturally but do not rush.

## Pronunciation for the oral

### Deliver, do not recite

- Speak in phrases, not word by word.
- Pause at commas and full stops.
- Vary your intonation: a question rises, a statement falls.
- Keep going after a mistake. Self-correct once, then continue.

### Confidence habits

- Start with a full sentence, not a one-word answer.
- Slow down slightly when you use a new structure.
- Use `bueno`, `pues` or `déjame pensar` to buy time instead of silence.
- If you do not understand, ask: `¿Puede repetirlo, por favor?` or `¿Qué significa ...?`
- Do not switch to English. Paraphrase: `No sé la palabra exacta, pero es algo que ...`

### Five-minute pronunciation routine

1. Choose one paragraph from a model answer.
2. Read it aloud slowly, marking the stressed syllable in long words.
3. Record yourself once.
4. Listen for flat vowels, missing accents and rushed endings.
5. Record again, slightly faster.

Do this three times a week. The goal is clarity and confidence, not a perfect Spanish accent.

## Spelling traps in writing

- `b` / `v`: `iba`, `vivo`, `tuve`, `estuve`.
- `c` / `s` / `z`: `gracias`, `cena`, `zapato`, `esperanza`.
- `g` / `j`: `gente`, `jugar`, `trabajo`, `viaje`.
- `h`: `hola`, `hacer`, `ahora`, `hoy`; but `a`, `e`, `o` without h.
- `ll` / `y`: `llamar`, `llegar`, `ayer`, `hoy`.
- Double consonants are rare: `perro`, `carretera`, `acción`, `opción`; not `hablar` with double l.
- Accents on question words: `¿Qué?`, `¿Cómo?`, `¿Dónde?`, `¿Cuándo?`, `¿Por qué?`.
- `porque` (because), `por qué` (why), `por que` (for which), `porqué` (reason).

## A final check before the oral

- Did you pronounce the vowels clearly?
- Did you stress the correct syllable in long words?
- Did you keep going after a mistake?
- Did you ask for repetition when needed?
- Did you use a phrase to buy time instead of stopping?

Clear, confident Spanish with a few errors scores better than a rushed answer that the examiner cannot follow.
