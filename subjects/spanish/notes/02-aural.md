# Listening: 80 marks in about 40 minutes

The aural paper is worth **80 marks** — 20% of the final grade. It is sat after the written paper and lasts about 40 minutes. There are **seven sections**, and each section is heard **three times**, with a pause during the second hearing.

This is not a test of whether you understand every word. It is a test of whether you can find the answer, write it accurately and keep moving.

## The seven sections

The exact texts change every year, but the format is consistent. Expect a mix of:

- a short announcement or public information message
- a description of a person, place or situation
- a dialogue or interview
- a weather report
- a news item
- a longer conversation with several questions
- a section that requires you to identify opinions, reasons or details

The SEC listening paper is answered **in English**. The 2024, 2025 and 2026 schemes all state that all answers must be in English; if the entire test or section is answered in Spanish, the examiner marks it to the scheme and awards **half of the marks gained**. Answer in short, clear English even when the audio is in Spanish.

Recent papers have used a consistent mark pattern: **9 + 12 + 13 + 13 + 13 + 8 + 12 = 80**. The early sections are usually shorter announcements or interviews; the weather section is usually 8 marks; the final news item is usually 12.

## The three-hearing method

### First hearing: gist

Read the questions before the audio starts. Underline the key words: names, numbers, places, times and question words. On the first hearing, do not try to write everything. Identify the topic and answer the questions you are sure about.

### Second hearing: detail

Use the pause to look at what is still blank. On the second hearing, listen for the specific detail that answers each question. Write a short answer immediately; you can tidy it later.

### Third hearing: check

Use the final hearing to confirm numbers, times, names and negations. Check that your answer actually answers the question. A common mistake is writing a true detail that does not match the question asked.

## Question types and how to handle them

| Question type | What to listen for | Exam tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Numbers, prices, times | digits, `euros`, `a las ...`, `media`, `cuarto` | Write digits as you hear them; check `quince` vs `cincuenta`. |
| Names and places | spelling, cities, countries | Copy the spelling carefully; accents matter less than recognisable names. |
| Reasons | `porque`, `ya que`, `debido a` | The reason often comes after the opinion, not before it. |
| Opinions | `creo que`, `pienso que`, `me parece` | Distinguish the speaker's view from the other person's. |
| Weather | `sol`, `nubes`, `lluvia`, `viento`, `temperaturas` | Weather reports often give tomorrow's forecast after today's. |
| Directions | `a la derecha`, `todo recto`, `la segunda calle` | Draw a tiny map while listening. |
| True/false/not given | exact wording | Do not mark true just because a word from the statement appears. |
| Gap fill | one or two words | Keep the answer short and grammatically fitted to the gap. |

## Listening traps

### Numbers

Spanish numbers can sound similar under pressure:

| Number | Spanish | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 | quince | vs `cincuenta` (50) |
| 16 | dieciséis | stress on the final syllable |
| 50 | cincuenta | vs `quince` |
| 60 | sesenta | vs `setenta` (70) |
| 70 | setenta | vs `sesenta` |
| 80 | ochenta | vs `ochenta y ...` |
| 90 | noventa | vs `noventa y ...` |

Prices and times often include `y media`, `y cuarto`, `menos cuarto`. Write the time in digits first and convert later if needed.

### Sound and spelling

- `b` and `v` sound the same in most varieties.
- `c` before `e`/`i`, and `z`, often sound like `th` in Spain but like `s` in Latin America.
- `ll` and `y` are usually close to an English `y`.
- The letter `h` is silent.
- Accents change stress and sometimes meaning: `esta` (this) vs `está` (is), `si` (if) vs `sí` (yes).
- Final consonants can be soft; use the context to identify words.

### Negation and opinion

Listen for `no`, `nunca`, `nadie`, `nada`, `tampoco`, `ni ... ni`. A sentence with a positive word may still be a negative answer. Similarly, `no creo que sea` is a negative opinion, not a positive one.

## A weekly listening routine

1. Do one past aural section under exam conditions: read the questions, listen three times, then stop.
2. Check your answers, but do not read the transcript yet.
3. Listen once more with the transcript. Underline the exact words that give each answer.
4. Note every new word or phrase in a topic list, not a random list.
5. Re-listen two days later and see whether the details are automatic.

You can also use short Spanish news clips or podcasts for gist practice. Do not pause every five seconds; listen for the main idea first, then go back for detail. The exam rewards selective listening, not perfect transcription.
